# Bletchley Park: The Place and Its People

## The Estate

In May 1938, Admiral Sir Hugh Sinclair, head of MI6, bought the Bletchley Park estate for 7,500 pounds out of his own pocket. The intelligence budget wouldn't cover it, so he paid personally. The site was a Victorian mansion 47 miles north of London in Buckinghamshire, chosen for two practical reasons: it sat at a rail junction with direct connections to both Oxford and Cambridge, and a telephone repeater station at nearby Fenny Stratford provided the communications bandwidth a codebreaking operation would need. Being 50 miles from London also made it an unlikely bombing target.

It was the tenth site acquired by the intelligence services and was designated "Station X." The Government Code and Cypher School (GC&CS) moved there in August 1939, just before Germany invaded Poland. Alastair Denniston, head of GC&CS, oversaw the construction of the wooden huts that would become the organizational backbone of the operation.

The huts divided the work:

- **Hut 6** handled Army and Air Force Enigma decryption. Gordon Welchman set it up with four internal sections: the Registration Room (traffic analysis), the Crib Room (preparing menus for the Bombes), the Machine Room, and the Decoding Room, which used modified Typex machines.
- **Hut 3** translated, analyzed, and distributed the decrypts from Hut 6 as "Ultra" intelligence for military commanders.
- **Hut 8** decrypted Naval Enigma. Alan Turing headed it from February 1940.
- **Hut 4** translated and analyzed the naval decrypts from Hut 8 and worked on hand ciphers and Italian naval traffic.

The workflow was a two-stage pipeline: Hut 6 decrypted Army/Air Force traffic for Hut 3 to analyze, and Hut 8 decrypted naval traffic for Hut 4 to analyze. At peak operation, approximately 10,000 people worked at Bletchley Park. About 7,500 of them, roughly 75%, were women.

## The Poles

The story of Enigma-breaking starts in Warsaw, not Buckinghamshire.

In late 1932, Captain Maksymilian Ciezki of the Polish Cipher Bureau (Biuro Szyfrow) assigned a 27-year-old mathematician named Marian Rejewski to work on the German Enigma traffic. French intelligence had obtained daily key tables for September and October 1932 from Hans-Thilo Schmidt, a German cipher clerk spying under the codename "Asche." Using this material and permutation group theory, Rejewski reconstructed the Enigma's internal wiring without ever touching the machine.

By January 1933, a three-man team, Rejewski, Jerzy Rozycki, and Henryk Zygalski, was reading German military Enigma traffic. By January 1938, they were decrypting about three-quarters of intercepted messages.

They built machines and methods to keep pace. The Bomba (plural: bomby kryptologiczne) was an electromechanical device built with engineers from the AVA Radio Manufacturing Company in Warsaw, purpose-built to automate the search through rotor configurations. Six bombas covered the six possible orderings of three rotors. Zygalski invented a system of 26 perforated cardboard sheets that, when stacked and rotated, revealed daily key settings through the alignment of holes.

In December 1938, Germany added rotors IV and V. This multiplied the possible rotor orderings from 6 to 60. The Poles would have needed 54 more bombas they couldn't afford to build. With war approaching and resources exhausted, the Cipher Bureau decided to share everything.

On 25 July 1939, in the Kabacki Forest near the village of Pyry south of Warsaw, Rejewski, Rozycki, and Zygalski met with French and British intelligence representatives. They revealed their methods and handed over reconstructed Enigma replicas with full technical documentation. The British delegation, including Alastair Denniston, learned that the Poles were "much further forward than they were, both quantitatively and qualitatively." This transfer gave Bletchley Park a five-year head start.

After Poland fell, the three cryptologists escaped through Romania to France, where they continued working at a secret intelligence center codenamed "Cadix." On 9 January 1942, Rozycki boarded the SS Lamoriciere in Algiers, bound for Marseille. The ship changed course after receiving a distress signal from another vessel, ran out of coal in a storm near the Balearic Islands, and began taking on water. Of 272 passengers, 93 survived. Rozycki, age 33, drowned. His body was never recovered. A symbolic grave was established in the Church of Saints Peter and Paul in Krakow in 2019.

Rejewski survived the war and returned to Poland in 1946. Under communist rule, he was pushed into low-ranking bureaucratic jobs and kept his Enigma work secret from the authorities until 1976. He wrote his memoirs only after 1973. He died in Warsaw on 13 February 1980. Most of his recognition came after his death: the Grand Cross of the Order of Polonia Restituta, and a War Medal 1939-1945 from Great Britain.

## Alan Turing

Turing designed the British Bombe, the electromechanical machine that made industrial-scale Enigma decryption possible. (Its design was distinct from the Polish bomba, though it attacked the same problem.) He also invented Banburismus, a statistical technique for naval Enigma that used sequential analysis to narrow the possible rotor orders from 60 down to 2 or 3, saving enormous amounts of Bombe time. Banburismus depended on captured bigram substitution tables. Without them, naval Enigma was opaque.

The breakthrough came in early 1941, when German naval codebooks and cipher equipment were captured from trawlers and a U-boat. Hut 8, under Turing's direction, could then read Kriegsmarine traffic.

Joan Clarke worked in Hut 8 as one of the few women who held a full cryptanalyst position. She became the leading practitioner of Banburismus. She and Turing became engaged. He gave her a ring and introduced her to his family, though she chose not to wear the ring at work. Turing eventually broke off the engagement, telling her he was homosexual. She later said it had not been a complete surprise. They remained close friends until his death.

In January 1952, Turing was convicted of "gross indecency" in Manchester after admitting to a sexual relationship with another man. He was offered a choice between imprisonment and chemical castration, a one-year course of hormone injections. He chose the injections. On 7 June 1954, he was found dead at his home in Wilmslow, Cheshire. He was 41. The cause of death was cyanide poisoning, ruled a suicide.

In September 2009, Prime Minister Gordon Brown issued a formal apology: "The debt of gratitude he is owed makes it all the more horrifying that he was treated so inhumanely... We're sorry, you deserved so much better." On 24 December 2013, Turing was granted a posthumous royal pardon under the Royal Prerogative of Mercy. In 2017, the "Turing Law" posthumously pardoned thousands of other men convicted under the same historical legislation.

## Gordon Welchman

Welchman, a Cambridge mathematician, made two contributions. The first was technical: the diagonal board, a modification to Turing's Bombe that exploited plugboard symmetry (if A connects to G, then G connects to A). Turing's original design didn't wire this reciprocal relationship. Welchman's improvement reduced false stops by over 90%, turning codebreaking from an intermittent triumph into a continuous process.

The second contribution was organizational. Welchman built Hut 6 from scratch, designing the workflow that processed German Army and Air Force Enigma traffic at scale throughout the war. The Registration Room, the Crib Room, the Machine Room, the Decoding Room. He designed a system that turned individual cryptanalytic insights into a factory for intelligence production.

After the war, Welchman emigrated to the United States and worked as a defense consultant, including at the MITRE Corporation. In 1982, he published *The Hut Six Story*, the first detailed account of how Bletchley Park actually decrypted German communications at industrial scale. The reaction was immediate. GCHQ and the NSA revoked his security clearance, which cost him his consultancy at MITRE. He was forbidden from discussing the book or his wartime work. The loss was described as "devastating." He was harassed and isolated by both agencies until his death on 8 October 1985.

## Dilly Knox

Alfred Dillwyn "Dilly" Knox was one of Britain's leading codebreakers across both world wars. During the first, he served as chief codebreaker in the Admiralty, breaking the German navy's main flag code.

At Bletchley Park, Knox was known for his habits. He wrote endless rude memos about his superiors. He appeared to subsist on chocolate and coffee. He was a terrifying driver who recited Milton along country lanes, hands off the steering wheel for emphasis. When deep in thought, he occasionally tried to refill his pipe with sandwiches. He was reportedly incapable of finding the right door out of a room on the first attempt, regularly walking into store cupboards.

His most significant wartime achievement came in 1941, when he broke the Abwehr (German Secret Service) Enigma. The unit built around his work, Intelligence Service Knox (ISK), disseminated 140,800 Abwehr decrypts by war's end, including intelligence used in planning D-Day. Knox recruited and mentored several women codebreakers, notably Mavis Lever (later Batey) and Margaret Rock.

Knox was diagnosed with lymphoma during the war. When he became too ill to travel to Bletchley, he continued working from his home in Hughenden. He died on 27 February 1943, age 58.

## The Women

The Bombe machines were operated almost exclusively by Wrens (Women's Royal Naval Service), who worked around the clock in shifts at outstations including Eastcote, Stanmore, and Gayhurst. They set up the machines according to menus provided by the cryptanalysts, monitored operation, and recorded results. The work was physically demanding and repetitive, and any error could mean a lost decrypt.

Mavis Lever was 19 years old and working under Dilly Knox when, in late March 1941, she deciphered an Italian naval Enigma message revealing that the Italian fleet planned to attack a Royal Navy supply convoy off Greece. The intelligence enabled the British victory at the Battle of Cape Matapan on 28 March 1941, where three Italian heavy cruisers and two destroyers were sunk. Lever later married fellow codebreaker Keith Batey and, decades later, wrote a biography of Knox titled *Dilly: The Man Who Broke Enigmas*. She died in 2013.

Joan Clarke's work in Hut 8 went beyond Banburismus. She held the same Deputy Head position as male colleagues with equivalent skill, though she was initially hired at a lower pay grade than men with the same qualifications, a situation that required bureaucratic workarounds. She and her colleagues performed Banburismus for two years, stopping in August 1943 when faster Bombes made the technique unnecessary.

Margaret Rock worked alongside Knox on the Abwehr Enigma variants. Like most women's contributions at Bletchley, hers went largely unrecognized for decades.

## Tommy Flowers and Colossus

Tommy Flowers was a Post Office engineer at the Research Station in Dollis Hill, northwest London. When Bletchley needed a machine to attack the Lorenz SZ40/42 cipher, a high-level German teleprinter cipher used for strategic communications between the High Command (distinct from Enigma, which handled tactical traffic), Flowers proposed an electronic solution using vacuum tubes.

He spent eleven months designing and building Colossus, investing 1,000 pounds of his own money. Colossus Mk 1 was delivered to Bletchley in late December 1943 and operational by early February 1944. It was the first large-scale electronic computer. By war's end, ten Colossus machines were running. Colossus Mk 2 went operational on 1 June 1944, five days before D-Day, and helped Eisenhower confirm that the Germans had not detected the Normandy deception.

After the war, eight of the ten machines were dismantled. Flowers was ordered to burn his engineering drawings. He took them to the boiler room and did it. The two surviving machines were moved to GCHQ's new headquarters at Cheltenham, where they continued in service until the 1960s.

The government gave Flowers 1,000 pounds after the war, which did not cover his personal investment. He divided the money among his team, keeping about 350 pounds for himself. He received an MBE but no knighthood. When he later applied for a bank loan and described his computing expertise, he was turned down. The bank saw no evidence that he had any such experience.

## The Silence

Every worker at Bletchley Park signed the Official Secrets Act. Workers in one hut were forbidden from discussing their work with workers in another. Families had no idea what their children did.

The Ultra secret held for three decades. Not one of the 12,000-plus people who worked at Bletchley and its intercept stations disclosed it until the mid-1970s.

In 1974, Group Captain Frederick Winterbotham, who had been responsible for distributing and securing Ultra intelligence during the war, published *The Ultra Secret*. The book was released in America, beyond the reach of British D-Notices. It contained some inaccuracies but broke the fundamental story. Hundreds of books, articles, and documentaries followed.

The decades of silence cost people in quiet ways. Parents of Bletchley veterans died without knowing what their children had done in the war. Veterans described becoming so accustomed to not talking that even after permission was granted, they still couldn't bring themselves to discuss their work. Tommy Flowers couldn't explain his computing expertise to a bank. Rejewski worked menial jobs under a communist government that never knew what he'd accomplished. The women who operated the Bombes received no public acknowledgment until the 21st century.

Equipment was deliberately destroyed. Colossus machines broken up, Flowers' drawings burned, Bombe machines scrapped. The physical evidence of what had been accomplished was erased along with the silence.
